# Teaching International Economic Law after Generative AI and Geoeconomic Fragmentation

## A Source-Centred, Comparative and Simulation-Based Model for Legal Education

### Abstract

Legal education now faces two linked disruptions. Generative artificial intelligence weakens the evidential value of conventional take-home essays, because polished legal prose can be produced without the student having performed the underlying cognitive work. At the same time, geopolitical rivalry has altered the substance of many law subjects themselves. In international economic law, the central pedagogical challenge is no longer simply to teach the doctrine of the WTO, investment law, trade in services or international finance. It is to help students reason in a world in which sanctions, export controls, data sovereignty, supply chain security, industrial policy, carbon border measures and strategic rivalry increasingly shape legal argument.

This article develops a reflective case study of the redesign of "International Economic Law and Relations" (LW6167E), a postgraduate course at the School of Law, City University of Hong Kong. Drawing on the course syllabus, assessment instructions, presentation schedule, student project materials, a project source corpus curated through Google Drive and NotebookLM, and recent scholarship on generative AI, legal education, Chinese legal education, comparative law, decolonial international law and geoeconomics, the article argues for a "dual-disruption" model of legal education. The model combines a secure individual lane for verified doctrinal competence with an open, supervised and AI-integrated lane for collaborative research, transparent AI use, source verification, comparative positioning and oral defence. Its central contribution is to show that AI-resilient assessment should not be designed merely to police misconduct. In advanced legal subjects, it should become an epistemic apprenticeship in which students learn to use AI critically, verify legal claims against primary sources, understand law as situated in geopolitical conflict, and defend legal judgment in public.

### Keywords

Generative AI; legal education; international economic law; Chinese law; comparative law; geoeconomics; assessment; TWAIL; legal pedagogy; source literacy

## 1. Introduction: From AI Panic to Legal-Educational Redesign

The arrival of widely available generative artificial intelligence has forced legal educators to confront an uncomfortable fact: a text no longer reliably proves that its named student author has performed the research, reasoning and drafting that legal education is meant to cultivate. A competent prompt can now produce a plausible memorandum, essay, policy brief or case analysis. The resulting text may be coherent, well organized and fluent. It may also be shallow, fabricated, jurisdictionally imprecise or subtly wrong. The problem is not simply plagiarism. It is that conventional assessment often treats final prose as a proxy for the process of legal judgment. Generative AI has broken that proxy.

International Economic Law (IEL) faces a second disruption. The subject matter itself is changing. The late twentieth-century teaching model often presented IEL as a field of rule-based multilateral governance organized around trade liberalization, investment protection, international financial stability and the gradual judicialization of economic relations. That model is now inadequate. The contemporary landscape is marked by the weaponization of economic interdependence, the fragmentation of global supply chains, unilateral and plurilateral sanctions, strategic export controls, industrial subsidies, investment screening, digital trade conflicts, data localization, artificial intelligence governance, carbon border adjustment and renewed debates about development, sovereignty and the Global South.[^1]

These two disruptions interact. AI changes how students produce legal work; geopolitics changes what legally competent work must understand. A student who can ask a large language model to summarize GATT Article XXI still may not understand why the national security exception has become a central legal language of U.S.-China technology rivalry. A student who can generate a comparison of EU, U.S. and Chinese data governance models still may not grasp how each model reflects different constitutional commitments, industrial strategies and geopolitical anxieties. A student who can produce a polished essay on carbon border adjustment may still be unable to defend a legal position when challenged by a peer representing a state, investor, trading partner or Global South constituency.

This article argues that advanced legal education should respond by redesigning both curriculum and assessment around source-centred, comparative and performative legal judgment. The proposal is not to ban AI, nor to surrender learning to AI. It is to make AI use visible, accountable and educationally productive while preserving secure spaces in which individual competence can be verified. The article develops this argument through a case study of the redesign of LW6167E, "International Economic Law and Relations", a P6 postgraduate course offered by the School of Law at City University of Hong Kong.

The redesigned course replaces a conventional individual written assignment with a collaborative group research project, a mandatory AI Usage and Methodology Statement, iterative faculty supervision, oral simulations and a controlled final examination. It also reframes the syllabus from a largely trade-law-centred course into an interdisciplinary inquiry into IEL in a geoeconomic world. The course now places WTO law, investment law, international finance and digital trade alongside sanctions, export controls, U.S.-China technology rivalry, supply chain resilience, industrial policy, carbon border adjustment, digital currencies, state capitalism, the Belt and Road Initiative and Global South perspectives.[^2]

The article makes four contributions. First, it offers a conceptual account of "dual-disruption pedagogy": a model for teaching law when both the means of student production and the substance of legal ordering are unstable. Second, it specifies a "two-lane-plus" assessment architecture for law teaching: secure individual verification, open AI-integrated research, and live oral defence. Third, it contributes to comparative and Chinese legal education scholarship by showing how a Hong Kong-based postgraduate course can treat Chinese law and policy neither as local background nor as exotic exception, but as one site within contested global legal ordering. Fourth, it argues that AI literacy in legal education must be joined to source literacy, geopolitical literacy and ethical professional judgment.

## 2. The Dual Disruption: AI, Legal Work and Geoeconomic Legal Order

### 2.1 Generative AI and the collapse of text-as-evidence

Generative AI has made a long-standing weakness in legal education newly visible. Legal education has often over-relied on written products submitted outside controlled conditions. Such products are convenient to assign and grade, but they are incomplete evidence of learning. They tell us something about the final document. They tell us less about how the student identified sources, distinguished primary from secondary authority, tested counterarguments, resolved uncertainty, managed collaboration or exercised judgment under pressure.

The new technology intensifies that evidentiary gap. John Bliss argues that legal educators should prepare students for a profession in which generative AI is increasingly embedded in legal research, drafting and communication, while also teaching students to evaluate AI output, understand its limits and reflect on its responsible use.[^3] Empirical work on AI and legal assessment makes the point sharper. Early studies showed that AI systems could produce passing work on law school examinations, though performance varied by task and prompt quality.[^4] More recent studies suggest that large language models can assist with or even approximate certain evaluative tasks, including grading, especially when supplied with detailed rubrics.[^5] These findings do not imply that law teachers should outsource grading or that AI can replace legal judgment. They do imply that legal educators must stop assuming that conventional written products are sufficient evidence of independent legal understanding.

Professional ethics points in the same direction. UNESCO's guidance for generative AI in education and research calls for a human-centred approach to ethical validation, privacy, policy design and pedagogy.[^24] The American Bar Association's Formal Opinion 512 treats generative AI as a technology governed by ordinary professional obligations: competence, confidentiality, communication, candor, supervision and reasonable fees.[^6] For legal education, the implication is direct. Students must learn not merely how to prompt AI tools, but how to decide whether a tool is suitable, what information may be entered, how outputs should be verified, what must be disclosed, and when human judgment is non-delegable. AI literacy is therefore not an optional technological add-on. It is becoming part of professional formation.

Yet a simple prohibition is a poor solution. Prohibitions are difficult to enforce, risk encouraging covert use, and can deprive students of guided experience with tools they will encounter in practice. Equally, unrestricted use without redesign can hollow out learning. The better response is architectural. Courses should distinguish between tasks whose purpose is to verify individual competence under controlled conditions and tasks whose purpose is to develop professional capability under open, technology-rich conditions. This is the logic of the "two-lane" assessment approach now being adopted or discussed in several universities: a controlled lane for assurance of learning and an open lane for authentic, AI-aware learning.[^7]

### 2.2 Geoeconomic fragmentation and the changing substance of IEL

At the same time, IEL has become a less settled field to teach. It remains doctrinally grounded in treaties, institutions, cases and principles. But its animating controversies are no longer well captured by a simple liberalization narrative. The WTO's negotiating paralysis and dispute settlement crisis, U.S.-China strategic competition, sanctions practice, export controls, investment screening, the return of industrial policy and struggles over data sovereignty have all shifted the field's centre of gravity. The concept of "weaponized interdependence" explains why networked global economic structures can become instruments of state coercion when powerful states control key hubs and chokepoints.[^8] Scholarship on post-neoliberal trade law similarly suggests that international trade law must now confront development, inequality, state capacity and democratic contestation rather than assume a universal policy preference for market liberalization.[^9]

This shift has pedagogical consequences. Traditional IEL teaching often trained students to identify applicable treaty provisions, apply interpretive methods and analyze precedent. Those skills remain essential. But they are now insufficient. Students also need to understand why states invoke national security, how economic measures are framed as trade regulation or industrial strategy, how legal claims operate in parallel with diplomatic bargaining, and how different legal systems and political economies shape what actors regard as legitimate. In other words, IEL teaching must become comparative, interdisciplinary and geopolitical without abandoning doctrinal discipline.

This is particularly important in Hong Kong and Chinese legal education. Chinese law is not merely a domestic legal system to be taught in isolation. It is increasingly part of global legal contestation: WTO accession and trade remedies, data governance and cyber-sovereignty, export controls, foreign investment regulation, state-owned enterprises, the Belt and Road Initiative, national security, and debates over foreign-related rule of law all place Chinese legal materials in comparative and international settings. Existing scholarship on Chinese legal education has emphasized globalization, practical skills, comparative training and the need to adapt legal education to changing professional and governance demands.[^10] The present challenge is to extend that agenda into the AI and geoeconomic era.

### 2.3 The missed opportunity in "AI-proofing"

Many immediate responses to generative AI have been framed around academic integrity: detection, prohibition, declarations, invigilation and punishment. Integrity matters. But if the central question is "How can we stop students from using AI?", legal education misses the deeper opportunity. The more valuable question is: "What forms of legal work should students now be required to perform so that AI becomes an object of critical practice rather than a substitute for learning?"

In advanced international and comparative law subjects, the answer should include source verification, multi-perspectival reasoning, oral defence, collaboration, transparent method and ethical judgment. AI can assist with these tasks, but it cannot complete them educationally unless students are required to show how they used it, how they checked it, how they revised it, and how they defended their conclusions when challenged. This is why the course redesign examined here treats AI and geopolitics together. The aim is not to design an assignment that AI cannot touch. The aim is to design a learning environment in which students cannot succeed by delegating judgment to AI.

## 3. Methodology and Case Context

This article is a reflective, source-grounded case study. It does not claim to present a controlled empirical evaluation of learning gains. Rather, it develops a theoretically informed account of a course redesign based on documentary evidence, teaching practice and analysis of student outputs. The sources include the 2025/26 LW6167E syllabus, the course's continuous assessment instructions, the group presentation schedule, selected student group reports and AI methodology statements, the Google Drive folder "International Economic Law and Relations" within the AC-Teaching folder, and the project source corpus curated through the "Teaching Law" NotebookLM notebook and the local research website built for this project.[^11]

The use of student materials is treated cautiously. The article refers to project topics, assessment design and recurring patterns in student reflection. It does not identify students by name or treat student reports as data from a formal human-subjects study. Any later journal submission should be checked against the author's institutional ethics requirements, particularly if more detailed student quotations or performance claims are added. For the present article, student work is used to illustrate pedagogical design and the kinds of learning activity that the redesigned course elicited.

The institutional context is important. LW6167E is a postgraduate English-medium course at City University of Hong Kong. The 2025/26 syllabus describes the course as a critical and comprehensive examination of IEL amid a paradigm shift in the global order. It remains grounded in the Bretton Woods institutions, the WTO, the IMF and the World Bank, but gives significant attention to geoeconomic competition, technological disruption and the green transition. Its stated learning outcomes include comprehension of the framework of IEL across trade, investment, finance and data movement; interpretation of legal texts and cases; understanding of international economic organizations and dispute settlement; analysis of the interplay between national regulation and international economic law; understanding of cutting-edge issues; and independent research using advanced legal technologies, including AI.[^12]

The course assessment architecture is equally important. Continuous assessment is 40 percent: a 30 percent research project or equivalent coursework component and a 10 percent class participation component. The final examination is 60 percent. The syllabus states that, where university facilities are available, students take a computer-based examination in computer labs with access to files, programs and the internet blocked.[^13] The design therefore already contains the basic separation between verified individual assessment and open developmental work. The pedagogical reform described in this article strengthens that separation and makes it explicit.

## 4. From Course Reform to "Two-Lane-Plus" Legal Assessment

The continuous assessment instructions explain the reform in plain terms. Generative AI tools such as ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude and DeepSeek can produce essays that, under the instructor's marking standards, may typically reach a B to B+ range when prompted well. The instructor therefore replaced the individual written assignment with a group research project designed to develop critical thinking, collaboration, real-world problem-solving and responsible use of technology.[^14]

This move should not be understood as a retreat from writing. The group report remains substantial. Rather, the reform changes what the written report is evidence of. It becomes one element in a wider chain of work: topic selection, source gathering, legal mapping, AI experimentation, verification, supervision, presentation, peer questioning and reflection. The written product is no longer asked to carry the entire evidentiary burden of assessment.

The resulting architecture can be described as "two-lane-plus." It uses two lanes, but adds a third element that is especially important for legal education: live defence.

| Component | Assessment Function | AI Position | Legal-Educational Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Controlled final examination | Secure verification of individual doctrinal competence and analytical reasoning | Restricted or unavailable under controlled conditions | Ensures that each student can reason without delegating core legal analysis |
| Collaborative research project | Open, developmental, authentic legal and policy work | Permitted, disclosed and audited through an AI Usage and Methodology Statement | Builds research, collaboration, source verification and responsible AI practice |
| Oral simulation and Q&A | Live professional performance and public defence | AI may assist preparation but cannot substitute for real-time response | Tests advocacy, negotiation, strategic judgment and the ability to answer challenge |
| Peer evaluation and supervision | Accountability and formative correction | AI use discussed as part of process | Reduces free-riding, supports reflection and catches errors before final submission |

The "plus" matters. Two-lane assessment can distinguish controlled from uncontrolled tasks, but law teaching also requires performance. Lawyers must speak, listen, negotiate, respond to hostile questions, revise positions, manage uncertainty and defend advice before others. Oral simulation re-centres these capacities. It also changes the role of AI. AI may help students prepare background, map issues, rehearse arguments or identify counterarguments. But it cannot by itself perform the live professional act of defending a position under questioning.

The course therefore develops four literacies.

First, source literacy. Students must distinguish treaty text, cases, state communications, institutional documents, scholarly commentary, policy reports, news and AI summaries. They must learn which source can support which claim.

Second, AI verification literacy. Students must disclose AI use, explain prompts and verify outputs. This shifts AI from a hidden shortcut to an object of legal method.

Third, comparative and geopolitical literacy. Students must understand that legal arguments are made from positions. A claim about data flows, subsidies, rare earths, investment screening or carbon tariffs looks different when made by China, the United States, the European Union, a developing state, an investor, a regulator or a tribunal.

Fourth, oral professional literacy. Students must translate research into real-time legal performance. In this respect the course draws on a long legal-education tradition that criticizes the gap between classroom doctrine and lawyering competence. The Carnegie Report famously urged legal education to integrate legal analysis, practical skill and professional identity rather than treating "thinking like a lawyer" as sufficient.[^15] Clinical and best-practices scholarship likewise emphasizes active, contextual and reflective learning.[^16]

## 5. Rebuilding the Syllabus: IEL as a Geoeconomic Laboratory

The syllabus redesign is not merely a change in topics. It changes the implicit theory of the field. The older model of the course, reflected in earlier course documents, emphasized WTO doctrine, trade remedies, dispute settlement, regional trade agreements and traditional legal analysis. The 2025/26 syllabus retains doctrinal foundations but places them within a broader account of economic order under stress.

| Dimension | Traditional IEL Teaching Baseline | Reformed 2025/26 Model |
|---|---|---|
| Core field image | Multilateral trade and investment law as rule-based economic ordering | IEL as contested governance under geoeconomic rivalry, technological disruption and climate transition |
| Dominant materials | WTO agreements, trade remedy doctrine, dispute settlement cases, investment law basics | WTO, IMF and World Bank foundations plus sanctions, export controls, industrial policy, digital trade, data governance, CBDCs, CBAM, critical minerals, state capitalism and Global South issues |
| Pedagogical role of China | Important trading state and source of examples | Central comparative and geopolitical actor in global legal contestation |
| Assessment premise | Written coursework can evidence independent research and analysis | Written work must be joined to disclosure, verification, supervision, oral defence and secure individual assessment |
| Professional skill focus | Doctrinal analysis and written explanation | Doctrinal analysis plus source verification, AI governance, negotiation, advocacy, strategic comparison and collaborative problem-solving |

This table captures the larger shift. IEL is taught not as a closed body of rules but as an argumentative field in which doctrine, power and institutional design interact. Students still need to know GATT Articles I, III, XI, XXI and XX; the SCM Agreement; GATS; investment treaty standards; and the architecture of dispute settlement. But they must also understand why those rules are invoked in conflicts over semiconductors, electric vehicles, digital services, investment screening, climate transition, critical minerals and monetary infrastructure.

The redesigned syllabus thus makes geopolitical rivalry a subject of legal education rather than a background condition. It also makes comparative law unavoidable. A digital trade problem may require students to compare the EU's rights-based data protection model, the U.S. market-oriented model and China's cyber-sovereignty and data security framework. An investment screening problem may require comparison between CFIUS, EU foreign direct investment screening, Chinese national security review, and treaty-based investment protection. A carbon border problem may require students to compare the EU's climate policy, WTO non-discrimination rules, Global South development claims and domestic industrial policy.

This matters for Chinese legal education. Comparative legal education in and about China has often been framed as a question of legal transplant, globalization or practical skills. Those remain important themes. But the geoeconomic era requires something more: students must learn to analyze Chinese law and policy as both domestic legal material and as part of transnational legal contestation. The question is not "How is China different?" but "How do Chinese legal positions, institutions and strategies interact with other legal orders in contested global regimes?"

## 6. The Group Research Project: From Assignment to Apprenticeship

The group project requires students to identify and analyze a concrete contemporary issue in IEL and international relations faced by states, international organizations or multinational corporations. The continuous assessment instructions list examples: WTO dispute settlement crisis, unilateral tariffs, economic sanctions, export controls, semiconductor restrictions, GATT Article XXI, ISDS reform, foreign investment screening, green transition, cross-border data flows, digital service taxes, AI regulation in trade agreements, CBAM, green subsidies and climate finance disputes.[^17]

The deliverables are deliberately layered. Each group submits a report of up to 5,000 words, an AI Usage and Methodology Statement of up to 500 words, a one-hour in-class presentation followed by Q&A, and a peer evaluation. The AI statement is crucial. It prevents AI from being treated as either forbidden magic or invisible labour. Students must disclose how AI tools were used for brainstorming, research, drafting, editing or translation; what prompts were used; and how outputs were verified against primary legal sources.[^18]

The supervision loop turns this structure into apprenticeship. Rather than receiving a topic, disappearing for weeks and submitting an essay, groups meet the instructor during the process. They present topics, outlines, sources, preliminary arguments and AI-use logs. The instructor can then intervene when students rely on hallucinated authorities, misread treaty provisions, confuse policy sources with law, or treat AI summaries as authority. This kind of feedback is not merely corrective. It teaches students how legal knowledge is made.

The design also responds to the problem of uneven AI benefits. AI may help weaker students by giving accessible explanations and initial structures. It may also harm learning when students accept fluent output without understanding. Student reports from the course reflect both sides. Some groups used AI to identify topics, generate initial summaries of unfamiliar fields, translate or simplify complex materials, organize legal arguments and rehearse debate formats. The same reports also show the need for source checking, deeper doctrinal specificity, more direct engagement with treaty provisions and more flexible oral response.[^19] That pattern supports the central claim of this article: AI-integrated learning must be supervised, source-linked and performed.

## 7. Student Projects as Evidence of a New Pedagogical Field

The presentation schedule and student reports show a striking range of contemporary problems: China-EU countervailing duties on electric vehicles, supply chain disruption, cross-border data flows among China, the United States and the European Union, CBAM, China-EU digital taxation, U.S. tariffs on China, White Plan and Keynes Plan debates, DS611 and the MPIA, critical minerals, U.S.-China trade policy review, the Northwest Passage, DS641 Canada tariff-rate quotas, the Dutch takeover of Nexperia, reciprocal tariffs and investment arbitration simulations.[^20] These topics are not random. They represent a field in transition.

### 7.1 Trade disputes and geoeconomic statecraft

The trade-disputes cluster forced students to confront the instability of WTO legalism in an age of national security. Projects on U.S. tariffs, Canada tariff-rate quotas, China-EU electric vehicle countervailing duties and semiconductor-related restrictions required students to map the difference between ordinary protectionism, industrial policy, supply chain security and security exceptions. Students could not simply recite non-discrimination. They had to ask whether a formally neutral measure has discriminatory effects, whether a tariff surcharge violates bound rates, whether a quota is a de facto quantitative restriction, and how Article XXI should be read after recent disputes over national security.

The pedagogical value of this cluster lies in the movement from doctrine to strategy. Students learned that legal argument in IEL is not merely syllogistic. It is also narrative. States frame themselves as rule defenders, victims of protectionism, guardians of national security or protectors of industrial resilience. In one student report on a U.S.-China trade policy review simulation, the teams represented China and the United States and debated data governance, technology subsidies, intellectual property, agricultural product safety and rare earth resources. The report reflected a sophisticated awareness that each side needed not only legal claims but also agenda control, evidence, counter-narrative and rhetorical discipline.[^21]

### 7.2 Investment screening, regulatory autonomy and strategic sectors

The investment cluster exposed students to the tension between investor protection and public regulatory authority. Traditional investment law teaching often starts with fair and equitable treatment, expropriation and investor-state arbitration. The reformed course keeps those concepts but situates them in a world of national security screening, green transition and strategic sectors. Students must ask how investment law responds when states screen foreign investment in semiconductors, energy infrastructure, data platforms or critical minerals; when climate policy disrupts legitimate expectations; or when investor protection collides with public interest regulation.

This is an important comparative-law exercise. It requires students to compare treaty standards, domestic screening mechanisms, arbitral approaches and political economy. It also helps them see that "regulatory autonomy" is not a slogan. It is a legal and institutional problem that depends on treaty text, domestic authority, evidence of public purpose, proportionality, compensation rules and the credibility of state explanation.

### 7.3 Digital trade, data governance and AI sovereignty

The digital-economy cluster is particularly valuable for teaching Chinese law, comparative law and international law together. Cross-border data flow disputes require students to compare legal models: EU data protection and fundamental rights; U.S. sectoral, market-oriented and national security approaches; Chinese data security, personal information protection, cyber-sovereignty and important-data regimes. Students must also understand how digital trade chapters in agreements such as CPTPP, USMCA and DEPA structure the legal vocabulary of data movement, localization and exceptions.

AI makes this cluster more complex. Data is not only regulated; it is also an input into machine learning. States therefore treat data governance as trade policy, industrial policy, national security policy and human-rights policy at once. This creates an ideal setting for students to learn comparative legal reasoning. The same legal measure can be described as privacy protection, national security, censorship, market access restriction, industrial strategy or digital sovereignty depending on viewpoint. The educational aim is not to force students into one characterization. It is to train them to identify the legal consequences of competing characterizations.

### 7.4 Climate, carbon borders, critical minerals and the Global South

The climate and sustainability cluster connects trade doctrine with distributive justice. CBAM projects require students to analyze MFN, national treatment, border adjustment, Article XX environmental exceptions and the relationship between climate policy and development. Critical minerals projects require them to consider export controls, resource conservation, supply chain security, green transition and the strategic value of rare earths. These are not marginal topics. They increasingly define the legal politics of the global economy.

This cluster also opens the door to TWAIL and decolonial approaches. Scholarship on decolonizing the international law curriculum asks legal educators to examine how dominant knowledge hierarchies are reproduced through teaching.[^22] Recent TWAIL commentary on AI governance similarly warns that AI may reproduce structural inequalities through data extraction, infrastructure dependence and regulatory models shaped by the Global North.[^23] When students study CBAM, data governance, AI regulation or critical minerals, they should therefore ask who benefits from a rule, who bears compliance costs, whose knowledge counts, and whose legal arguments are treated as universal rather than positional.

## 8. Contributions to Legal Education Theory

### 8.1 From integrity policing to epistemic apprenticeship

The first contribution is conceptual. The article reframes the AI challenge in legal education as an epistemic problem rather than merely an integrity problem. Academic integrity rules remain necessary, but they cannot by themselves produce legal competence. The real task is to design learning environments in which students must show how legal knowledge is made: how sources are found, how AI outputs are tested, how uncertainty is handled, how law is compared across systems, and how claims are defended before others.

This reframing moves legal education away from a narrow "AI-proofing" mentality. A task is not educationally good simply because AI performs it poorly. Nor is a task educationally bad simply because AI can assist with it. The question is whether the task requires students to exercise judgment that is visible, assessable and connected to professional practice. In LW6167E, the answer comes from combining disclosure, verification, supervision, collaboration and oral defence.

### 8.2 A legal version of the two-lane model

The second contribution is architectural. The two-lane model offers a helpful distinction between controlled assessment and AI-enabled developmental assessment. But law needs a discipline-specific version. Legal education should not stop at "closed exam plus open assignment." It should add structured performance: oral defence, negotiation, client advice, tribunal argument, mediation, treaty drafting, policy briefing or simulation. The resulting model can be summarized as follows:

1. Secure doctrinal lane: students demonstrate individual competence in controlled conditions.
2. Open research lane: students use available tools, including AI, but disclose and verify their method.
3. Performance lane: students defend, negotiate or apply their legal position in real time.

This structure aligns with the Carnegie Report's call to integrate legal analysis, practical skill and professional identity. It also addresses the practical reality that legal work is increasingly collaborative, technologically mediated and public-facing.

### 8.3 Comparative law as a method, not an ornament

The third contribution concerns comparative legal education. Comparative law is too often added to courses as supplementary information: a foreign example, a contrast, a brief note on another system. In a geoeconomic course, comparison becomes method. Students cannot analyze digital trade without comparing data governance models. They cannot analyze investment screening without comparing domestic security review regimes. They cannot analyze Chinese trade policy without comparing U.S., EU, WTO and Global South positions. They cannot understand AI governance without comparing regulatory capacity, infrastructure, data access and political economy.

For Chinese law teaching, this matters. Chinese legal materials should not be presented only as a distinct national system or a set of political controversies. They should be taught as legal materials that participate in global argument. This approach avoids two errors: treating Chinese law as simply derivative of Western models, and treating it as so exceptional that ordinary comparative method cannot apply. Instead, it asks students to analyze Chinese law in relation to transnational legal regimes, competing state strategies and the shifting professional demands of cross-border practice.

### 8.4 AI literacy as professional and ethical formation

The fourth contribution is ethical. AI literacy in law is not merely a technical skill. It is part of professional formation. Students must learn that AI use can implicate confidentiality, competence, candor, supervision, bias, access to justice and cost. They must also learn that AI outputs may be persuasive without being authoritative. This is especially important in international and comparative law, where models may overgeneralize, fabricate citations, flatten jurisdictional differences, reproduce dominant-language biases or miss recent legal developments.

The AI Usage and Methodology Statement is therefore more than an integrity declaration. It is a pedagogical instrument. It asks students to reconstruct their workflow, identify where AI entered the process, and explain how they preserved legal responsibility. Properly used, such statements can teach metacognition: students learn to see their own research process as something that can be examined, improved and defended.

### 8.5 Source infrastructure as pedagogy

The fifth contribution arises from the project infrastructure itself. The research project supporting this article used a Google Drive corpus, a NotebookLM "Teaching Law" notebook and a local source-centred website to curate teaching-law sources. This matters because AI-integrated pedagogy depends on source environments. If students use general chatbots in a vacuum, they may learn prompt fluency without source discipline. If they work from curated but contestable source collections, they can learn how arguments depend on evidence.

For law schools, this suggests a practical agenda. Courses should build local, source-centred workspaces: primary legal texts, cases, institutional documents, policy materials, selected scholarship, student guidance, assessment rubrics and AI-use protocols. Students should learn to ask AI tools questions against these materials, but they should also learn to check the answer. The future is not simply "AI in legal education." It is governed, source-aware, accountable AI in legal education.

## 9. Risks, Limits and Conditions for Transfer

The model is promising but not costless. It requires time, supervision and institutional support. Group projects can create free-riding unless peer evaluation and supervision are serious. Oral simulations can reward confidence over substance unless rubrics are clear. AI statements can become formulaic unless instructors read and discuss them. Controlled examinations can verify individual competence but may not capture the full range of professional skills. There are also equity concerns: students may have unequal access to better AI tools, different language abilities, different confidence in oral performance and different familiarity with global legal sources.

Privacy and confidentiality also matter. If students upload course materials, unpublished student work or sensitive documents into external AI tools, legal and institutional risks may arise. Legal education should therefore teach not only "how to use AI" but also "where not to use AI" and "what may not be entered." This connects student practice to professional duties.

The case-study nature of this article is another limitation. The article does not measure learning outcomes quantitatively. It does not compare grades before and after the redesign. It does not present systematic student interviews. Future research should do so. A stronger empirical study could examine student AI statements, compare quality of source use, evaluate oral performance, interview students about AI and geopolitical learning, and test whether the model improves retention, transfer and professional confidence.

Finally, the model must be adapted by field. A first-year contract course, a Chinese constitutional law seminar, an international arbitration clinic and an IEL course should not use identical designs. The transferable principle is not the specific set of topics. It is the architecture: verified individual competence, open source-centred AI use, explicit methodology, iterative feedback, comparative positioning and live defence.

## 10. Conclusion: Teaching Judgment in a Fragmented and Automated World

Generative AI has made it easier to produce legal text and harder to know what that text proves. Geopolitical rivalry has made legal subjects such as IEL more contested and harder to teach as stable doctrine. Together, these changes require more than policy statements about cheating or technology. They require curricular redesign.

The LW6167E reform offers one model. It treats AI not as an enemy of legal education, but as a condition under which legal education must become more rigorous. It treats geopolitics not as a distraction from legal doctrine, but as part of the reality in which legal doctrine now operates. It treats Chinese law, comparative law and international law not as separate silos, but as interacting sites of professional judgment.

The central lesson is simple but demanding: students should not be assessed only on the polish of the final text. They should be assessed on the quality of their sources, the transparency of their method, the discipline of their AI use, the accuracy of their legal analysis, the sophistication of their comparative positioning, the ethics of their professional choices and their ability to defend a judgment before others. In the age of AI and geopolitical rivalry, the task of legal education is not to preserve old assignments in more secure form. It is to teach students how to think, verify, compare and argue when both the tools of legal work and the world of legal order are changing.
